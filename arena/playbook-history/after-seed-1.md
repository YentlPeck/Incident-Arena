# Investigation Playbook

## Setup and orientation
- Survey every available screen, tab, and menu before drawing conclusions; note which sources exist (messages, logs, access records, files, social posts, notes).
- Keep a running list of claims, each tagged with its source and whether it is firsthand, secondhand, or anonymous.
- Record discoveries as soon as found; the number of findable items is usually capped, so track progress toward it.
- Re-read the task prompt for what the final report must contain (culprit, method, supporting evidence) and collect for each part.

## Establishing who
- Build a timeline of the key event first, then ask who could physically or digitally have been present at that moment.
- Check each suspect's alibi against independent records, not only their own statements.
- Automatic replies, travel confirmations, sign-in locations, and public posts can show someone was elsewhere.
- When an account was used, separate the account owner from the person actually operating it.
- Ask how the operator could have obtained access: shared notes, written credentials, reused secrets, or unattended devices.
- Check who viewed or opened the access-enabling material shortly before the event.

## Establishing how
- Trace the item from origin to where it surfaced; identify each hop and the time gap between hops.
- Unique markers embedded in a leaked copy (tags, marks, identifiers) tie it to a specific download or session; match them to access records.
- Match location records (door readers, device sign-ins, session logs) against the timeline to place one person at one device.
- Compare clocks across sources; confirm they use the same reference before comparing times.

## Evidence quality
- Prefer machine-generated records (access logs, system logs, audit trails) over narrative statements.
- Treat anonymous tips as hypotheses to test; look for what the tip gains and whether records contradict it.
- A strong case combines at least two independent sources that converge: one placing the person, one showing the action.
- Look for evidence that excludes alternatives, not only evidence that supports the favored suspect.
- Notice absences: missing log entries, gaps, or records that should exist but do not.

## Working the interface
- Open every item in lists, including attachments, replies, and linked pages; details often sit one level down.
- Use search and filter features for names, identifiers, and device labels once you have a lead.
- Scroll to the end of long lists; relevant entries may be below the fold.
- Zoom or re-capture when text is small; transcribe exact identifiers and times before comparing.
- Do not repeat the same action without new information; if stuck, switch to an unexplored source.

## Reporting
- State the culprit, the method, and the specific evidence items by their source and content.
- Cite the pair of records that together prove the claim, such as a location record plus an activity record.
- Mention and rebut the false lead explicitly, with the record that contradicts it.
- Keep the report factual and short; avoid speculation beyond what records show.
- Submit only after each required part has a supporting item; then verify the submission was accepted.

## Process habits
- Do not assume any one kind of person or method is always responsible; let the records decide.
- Revisit early assumptions when a new record conflicts with them.
- Note time gaps between related events; short gaps often indicate a single actor.
- Before filing, list the open questions and confirm none would change the conclusion.
