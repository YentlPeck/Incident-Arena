# Studio Craft Notes

## Pixel art basics
- Pick a small palette before drawing anything; a limited set of colours keeps sprites readable and gives the whole game one recognisable look.
- Draw silhouettes first. If a character is recognisable as a solid shape, the details inside it will work too, and the player will spot it at a glance.
- Use one light direction for every sprite in a scene so that shading feels consistent from room to room and objects seem to share the same world.
- Avoid pillow shading, where an outline is simply darkened all the way round; it flattens the form instead of rounding it and makes sprites look puffy.
- Keep outlines one pixel wide and break them where the light hits, so shapes do not look like stickers pasted on top of the background.
- Hue-shift your ramps: shadows lean towards cool colours and highlights towards warm ones, which looks richer and livelier than pure darkening.
- Anti-alias sparingly. A few in-between pixels on long curves help; too many make the art look blurry and soft at its real size.
- Zoom out often. Pixel art is seen at its real size, and problems that hide at high zoom show up immediately at one hundred percent.

## Animation
- Start with key poses, then add in-betweens; a walk cycle of six to eight frames usually feels smooth enough for a small sprite.
- Exaggerate squash and stretch on jumps and landings, then pull it back until it stops looking rubbery but still feels bouncy and alive.
- Give idle animations a slow, subtle motion such as breathing or blinking, so characters feel alive while the player is thinking.
- Hold the first and last frame of an attack a little longer; anticipation and follow-through make hits feel strong and readable.
- Keep the feet planted during a walk cycle. Sliding feet are the most common reason a walk feels wrong, even when every frame looks fine.
- Reuse frames with small changes rather than drawing every frame from scratch; it saves time and keeps the style even across the game.

## Level design
- Teach one idea at a time. Introduce a new mechanic in a safe room before asking the player to use it under pressure or against enemies.
- Use landmarks so players can find their way back; a unique statue, fountain or tree is far easier to remember than another plain corridor.
- Let the level layout point the way: light, colour and open space draw the eye towards the next goal without any arrows or signs.
- Alternate tense and calm sections so players can breathe between challenges and enjoy the scenery for a moment.
- Hide optional secrets near the main path, not far away, so curious players are rewarded without getting lost or frustrated.
- Playtest with people who have never seen the level and watch silently; where they hesitate or turn around is where the design needs work.

## Sound and music
- Give every important action its own short sound so players can feel the result without looking, from opening a door to picking up a coin.
- Keep menu sounds soft; they are heard hundreds of times in one evening and quickly become tiring if they are loud or sharp.
- Loop music seamlessly by matching the end of a track to its start in key and tempo, so players never notice where it repeats.
- Lower the music a little when dialogue appears so the words stay clear, then bring it back up gently when the box closes.
- Use silence on purpose; a sudden quiet moment before a big room can be more dramatic than any sound effect.

## Writing for games
- Keep dialogue boxes short. Two or three lines at a time are easier to read than a wall of text, especially on a small screen.
- Let characters show who they are through what they do, not only through what they say about themselves.
- Give each important character a recognisable way of speaking, such as a favourite word or a short rhythm, so lines can be told apart without names.
- Write item descriptions that hint at a use or a little story, which makes collecting things more interesting and rewards curious players.
- Read every line out loud once; awkward sentences and accidental rhymes are much easier to hear than to see on a page.

## Game feel
- Add a tiny pause on impact when an attack connects; a few frames of freeze make hits feel heavy and satisfying.
- Use screen shake rarely and briefly so it stays meaningful, and always offer a setting to turn it off for players who dislike it.
- Give the jump some forgiveness: allow a jump a moment after leaving a ledge and remember a press made just before landing.
- Make the controls respond on the very first frame of a button press; delays are felt even when they are not consciously seen.
- Celebrate small wins with particles, sounds and colour so progress feels good, even in the quiet parts of the game.

## Interface
- Show only what the player needs right now; extra numbers and icons on screen compete for attention with the actual game.
- Use the same button for the same kind of action across every menu, so confirming and going back never surprise anyone.
- Make text large enough to read from a sofa when the game may be played on a television across the room.
- Offer colour-blind friendly options and never rely on colour alone to show important differences between items or teams.
- Let players change and review their settings at any time, not only on the title screen before the game has started.

## Working as a small team
- Keep a shared list of tasks with one owner each, so nothing is forgotten, nothing is done twice and everyone knows what is next.
- Show work early and often; feedback on a rough sketch is much cheaper than feedback on a finished, polished level.
- Cut features that do not support the core idea of the game, even good ones; a smaller game that is finished beats a large one that is not.
